## TL;DR

Working closely with a few customers is one of the best things a product team can do. It can also quietly shape the product around how those few customers happen to work. Doing more discovery isn't the whole answer. It helps to look for customers who work differently, ask why each step in a process exists, and separate what customers need from how their organization happens to do it.

## Deep isn't the same as broad

Most product people I know would say customer discovery is one of the most important parts of the job. I'd say the same. Watching how people actually work, and checking that a problem is real before building for it, is how you end up with something people use.

What I've noticed is a problem that shows up when discovery goes well, just with too few customers.

Early on, it's normal to work closely with a handful of customers or design partners. You get real workflows, honest feedback, and a place to test ideas. Over months, you get to know how those customers operate in a lot of detail.

That detail is useful. But after a while it's easy to forget that their way of working is one option among several, not the way the work has to be done.

## A need versus a workflow

Say we're building software for a process that involves collecting data, reviewing it, and approving it.

Our first customer has a clear workflow. A coordinator gathers the information, a technical specialist reviews it, and a manager signs off. We watch the process, talk to the people doing it, find where it's slow or painful, and build something that makes it easier. The customer is happy, and we've solved a real problem.

The next organization might not have a separate coordinator. Another might automate most of the review. Another might have regulatory steps the first one never deals with. They all want the same outcome, but they get there differently.

**Discovery shows us how people work. We also need to understand why they work that way.**

Some steps are there because the outcome requires them. Others are there because of staffing, internal policy, the systems they already have, or just history. If we can't tell those apart, we end up building one organization's habits into the product.

I wrote about how this shows up in demos, when a prospect asks why a step exists and the honest answer is "because our first customer did it that way," in [Whose Organizational Debt Are We Building Into Our Products?](/reflections/whose-organizational-debt-are-we-building-into-our-products/) Here I want to focus on what we can do earlier, during discovery itself.

## Pay attention to the differences

In discovery we usually look for patterns: the pain points that come up again and again, the needs everyone shares. That's how we decide what belongs in the product.

Differences deserve just as much attention. When two organizations solve the same problem in different ways, I want to know why. Different regulations? Different staffing? Different tolerance for risk? Is one more mature? Is one carrying complexity the other already got rid of?

The answers tell you which parts of a workflow are essential and which parts should flex.

This doesn't mean talking to dozens of customers before every decision. It means being deliberate about who you talk to. Two or three customers who work in meaningfully different ways will surface assumptions that ten similar customers never will. Often the most useful feedback comes from the customer who doesn't fit the model you've already built.

## Customers know their problems. The product is our job.

Customers are the experts on their own world. They know their constraints, their responsibilities, and what they're trying to get done, and that should shape what we build.

But it isn't their job to decide which parts of their current process become permanent parts of a product other companies will use. That's on the product team. We take what we learn, figure out what customers are actually trying to accomplish, and decide what should be standard, what should be configurable, and what should be left out.

Discovery gives us the evidence. Deciding what it means is our job.

## Depth and breadth

Close customer relationships are a real advantage. They show you the details and messiness of real problems in a way nothing else does.

But knowing a few customers deeply is different from knowing the market. You need the depth to understand the problem and the breadth to know which details are fundamental and which are specific to one organization.

It's easy to mistake familiarity for universality. **The real test of discovery isn't whether we understand how our first customers work. It's whether we understand the problem well enough to serve the ones who work differently.**
