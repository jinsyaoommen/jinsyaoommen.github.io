## TL;DR

Customer discovery is essential to building successful products, but working closely with a small number of customers can introduce unintended design bias. We can become very good at understanding how those customers operate and mistake their workflows for universal requirements. The challenge isn't simply doing more discovery. It's deliberately seeking different operating models, understanding why processes exist, and distinguishing underlying customer needs from organizational preferences.

## The hidden bias in customer discovery

We often talk about customer discovery as one of the most important parts of product development. And rightly so.

Understanding customers, observing how they work, and validating problems before building solutions are fundamental to creating products people actually need.

But I've been thinking about an interesting challenge, particularly when building SaaS platforms.

**What happens when we do customer discovery well, but with too narrow a set of customers?**

In the early stages of product development, it's common to work closely with a handful of customers or design partners.

These relationships are incredibly valuable. We get access to real workflows, detailed feedback, and opportunities to test our assumptions.

Over time, we develop a deep understanding of how these customers operate.

But there's a subtle risk.

We can become so familiar with their operating models that we stop recognizing them as one of several possible ways to solve a problem.

## The difference between a need and a workflow

Imagine we're building software to support a business process involving data collection, review, and approval.

Our initial customer has a well-defined workflow. A coordinator collects information, a technical specialist reviews it, and a manager provides final approval.

We observe the process, interview users, identify pain points, and design a solution that makes the workflow faster and easier.

Our customer is happy. We've solved a real problem.

But have we designed a workflow that works across the market, or have we optimized one organization's process?

Another organization might combine the coordinator and technical specialist roles. A third might automate most of the review. A fourth may have additional regulatory requirements.

All of them are trying to accomplish the same underlying outcome, but their workflows differ.

**Customer discovery tells us how people work. Good product discovery must also reveal why they work that way.**

Some workflow steps are fundamental to achieving the desired outcome. Others reflect staffing, internal policies, existing systems, or organizational history.

If we don't distinguish between them, we may inadvertently build those organizational assumptions into our product.

## When the demo reveals our assumptions

One of the most useful moments in product development can happen during a demonstration to a prospective customer.

We're explaining the product, walking through a workflow, and showing how users accomplish a task.

Then we reach a step that requires a particular role, approval, or organizational structure.

The prospective customer asks, "Why does it have to work that way?"

And we realize we don't have a compelling answer beyond the fact that this was how our original customer operated.

What seemed completely natural during development suddenly feels unnecessarily restrictive.

The problem wasn't that our initial customer gave us bad information.

The problem was that we treated a specific implementation of a workflow as though it were a universal requirement.

That moment can be a powerful reminder that **validating a solution with one customer doesn't necessarily validate it for a market.**

## Looking for differences, not just similarities

When we conduct discovery, we're often looking for patterns.

We want to identify common pain points and repeated needs because those patterns help us determine what belongs in the product.

But differences between customers can be just as valuable.

If two organizations solve the same problem differently, that difference deserves investigation.

Is one organization operating under different regulatory requirements? Do they have different staffing models? Are their risk tolerances different? Does one have a more mature process? Or is one carrying historical complexity that the other has already eliminated?

The answers help us separate what is essential from what is variable.

This doesn't mean interviewing dozens of customers before making every product decision.

It means being intentional about the diversity of perspectives we include.

Even a small number of customers with meaningfully different operating models can expose assumptions that might otherwise remain invisible.

And sometimes the most useful feedback comes from customers who don't fit the model we've already designed.

## Customers are experts in their problems, not necessarily in our product architecture

I think there's another important distinction here.

Customers are experts in their own environments. They understand their challenges, constraints, responsibilities, and desired outcomes.

Their experience should inform our product decisions.

But customers shouldn't have to determine which parts of their existing processes become permanent features of a SaaS platform.

That's the responsibility of the product team.

We need to understand what customers are trying to accomplish, identify the capabilities required, and make thoughtful decisions about which workflows should be standardized, configurable, or excluded from the product.

Customer discovery provides the evidence. Product design requires interpreting that evidence.

**Our responsibility isn't simply to reproduce what customers tell us they do. It's to understand the problem well enough to design something that serves customers beyond those we initially studied.**

## A final thought

Deep customer relationships are an enormous advantage in product development.

But depth of understanding and breadth of understanding are different things.

We need both.

The first helps us understand the details and complexity of real customer problems.

The second helps us determine which of those details are fundamental and which are specific to an organization.

As we build SaaS products intended to serve many customers, we should be careful not to confuse familiarity with universality.

**The real test of customer discovery isn't whether we understand how our first customers work. It's whether we understand the problem well enough to serve customers who work differently.**
