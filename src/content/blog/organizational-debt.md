I've spent much of my career in software development, where technical debt is a familiar concept.

We make tradeoffs. We take shortcuts. We build something quickly because there's a customer need, a deadline, or a business opportunity we don't want to miss.

And sometimes, that's absolutely the right decision.

But we also know those decisions have consequences. The shortcuts we take today can make tomorrow's changes harder, slower, and more expensive.

Good engineering teams understand this. They make technical debt visible, evaluate the tradeoffs, and intentionally invest in reducing it.

So why don't we treat organizational decisions the same way?

Because organizations accumulate debt too.

And I suspect we're carrying far more of it than we realize.

## Organizational Debt Is Everywhere

Think about how organizations evolve.

A team needs a better way to manage information, so someone creates a spreadsheet.

A department needs a new approval process, so a few extra steps get added.

Two teams need to coordinate, so we introduce another meeting.

A customer has a unique requirement, so we create a special workflow.

Someone builds an internal application because the existing enterprise system doesn't quite meet their needs.

Individually, these decisions make sense. They're often made by thoughtful, capable people trying to solve immediate problems.

But over time, those decisions accumulate.

We end up with multiple sources of truth, overlapping responsibilities, inconsistent processes, disconnected tools, and meetings that nobody remembers why we started.

Eventually, the organization becomes harder to navigate.

Getting something done requires knowing the right person, understanding an undocumented exception, or manually moving information between systems.

**That's organizational debt.**

It's the accumulated cost of decisions that solve immediate needs while creating future complexity, dependencies, and friction.

And much like technical debt, it's often invisible until it starts slowing us down.

## We Recognize Technical Debt. Why Not Organizational Debt?

In software engineering, we understand that code has a lifecycle.

We don't expect every line of code written ten years ago to remain the best solution today.

Technologies change. Business requirements evolve. Architectural decisions that once made sense may no longer serve us.

So we refactor.

We simplify.

We retire outdated systems.

We invest in improving the foundations that allow us to move faster in the future.

Now think about how we manage organizational processes.

When was the last time we intentionally refactored an approval process?

Or retired a recurring meeting because its original purpose no longer existed?

Or examined whether responsibilities that made sense five years ago still belong in the same teams?

Or consolidated tools and workflows that grew independently across departments?

We tend to add organizational structures and processes much more easily than we remove them.

And unlike technical debt, organizational debt rarely appears on a backlog.

There's no organizational debt register. No regular review of accumulated process complexity. No explicit investment in reducing the friction we've created.

Instead, we normalize it.

We call it coordination. Governance. Alignment. Or simply how things work around here.

## Organizational Debt Has Interest Payments Too

One of the most useful ways to think about technical debt is the idea of interest.

The original shortcut may save time, but every future change becomes a little more expensive because of it.

Organizational debt works the same way.

Consider a process that requires three unnecessary approvals.

Each approval might take only a few minutes. But across hundreds of decisions, multiple teams, and an entire year, the cost becomes significant.

And the cost isn't just the time spent approving something.

It's the waiting.

The context switching.

The follow-up messages.

The decisions that get delayed because someone isn't available.

The opportunities that never get pursued because the process feels too difficult.

Or consider two departments maintaining separate customer databases.

At first, each system serves its team's needs.

But eventually, someone needs a unified view of the customer. Now the organization must reconcile data, resolve inconsistencies, build integrations, and establish ownership.

The original decision may have been inexpensive.

The interest payments are not.

**Organizational debt compounds through coordination costs, slower decisions, duplicated effort, and reduced adaptability.**

It also eats into the time and energy we could be spending on something new.

## What Would It Mean to Refactor an Organization?

In software, refactoring means improving the internal structure of a system without changing its intended external behavior.

What would that look like for an organization?

Maybe it means eliminating three approval steps without reducing accountability.

Maybe it means consolidating customer information into a shared system rather than asking employees to maintain multiple records.

Maybe it means replacing recurring status meetings with better visibility into work.

Maybe it means clarifying decision rights so that teams can move without constantly escalating decisions.

Or investing in shared integration and orchestration capabilities so employees can build specialized workflows without recreating foundational systems.

The objective isn't simply to reduce the number of processes, tools, or meetings.

Some complexity is necessary. Governance, security, compliance, and coordination all serve important purposes.

The objective is to distinguish **complexity that creates value from complexity we've simply inherited.**

And then intentionally remove the latter.

## We Need an Organizational Debt Backlog

What if organizations treated organizational debt with the same discipline that mature engineering teams apply to technical debt?

We could start by making it visible.

Identify the processes that repeatedly slow teams down. The systems that require duplicate data entry. The unclear ownership that causes decisions to bounce between departments. The workarounds that have quietly become permanent.

Then evaluate the impact.

How much time are we spending maintaining these processes?

How often do they delay decisions?

How much rework do they create?

What opportunities are we missing because our organizational systems can't adapt quickly enough?

Not every item needs to be fixed immediately.

Just like technical debt, some organizational debt is an intentional tradeoff. We may choose to accept it because the cost of addressing it exceeds the benefit.

But that should be a conscious decision.

We should also create capacity to address it.

If every team is fully occupied delivering new initiatives, who is responsible for improving how the organization operates?

In engineering, we eventually learn that allocating all our capacity to new features while ignoring technical debt is unsustainable.

I think the same principle applies to organizations.

**We can't continuously add new initiatives, processes, and systems without investing in simplifying what already exists.**

## Organizational Debt Is a Leadership Responsibility

There's another important difference between technical debt and organizational debt.

Technical debt often has a relatively clear owner. An engineering team can identify a problem, prioritize it, and improve the codebase.

Organizational debt rarely belongs to just one team.

A cumbersome approval process may span finance, legal, operations, and technology.

Duplicated data may exist across sales, delivery, and customer success.

Unclear decision rights may be the result of years of organizational changes.

No individual team has the authority to resolve the entire problem.

That's why I believe organizational debt needs leadership attention.

Not necessarily another committee or governance process. That could create even more debt!

But a shared recognition that organizational complexity has a cost, and that reducing unnecessary complexity is a legitimate business investment.

Leaders should be asking not only what new capabilities we need to create, but also what existing complexity we can eliminate.

What can we stop doing?

What can we simplify?

What can we consolidate?

What can we automate?

And what decisions can we move closer to the people doing the work?

These questions deserve as much attention as the next strategic initiative.

## The Organizations That Move Fastest May Be the Ones That Simplify Best

We often associate organizational maturity with adding structure.

More processes. More controls. More systems. More coordination.

And certainly, growing organizations need structure.

But I wonder if the next stage of organizational maturity is learning how to continuously simplify.

To recognize when a process has outlived its usefulness.

To retire tools instead of endlessly adding new ones.

To invest in shared capabilities rather than solving the same problem repeatedly.

To make decision-making clearer rather than introducing another layer of oversight.

And to treat organizational complexity as something we actively manage rather than passively inherit.

AI will undoubtedly help us build and automate more things.

But building more things isn't necessarily the same as becoming a more effective organization.

Sometimes the greatest productivity improvement comes not from adding another tool, but from eliminating the need for it in the first place.

**We've learned that technical debt, left unmanaged, eventually slows software development. Organizational debt, left unmanaged, can slow an entire company.**

Maybe it's time we started managing both with the same intention.
